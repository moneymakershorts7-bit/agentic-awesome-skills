# Directing Grammar & Camera Choreography 🎬📐

Master guide for visual directing, spatial staging, blocking, lens choice, and camera movement.

---

## 1. Shot Sizes & Psychological Purpose

| Shot Size | Abbreviation | Framing | Directorial Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Extreme Wide Shot** | EWS | Subject is tiny in vast landscape | Establishes environment, loneliness, insignificance, epic scale |
| **Wide Shot / Master** | WS | Whole human body visible head-to-toe | Establishes spatial relationships, physical blocking, action geography |
| **Medium Long Shot** | MLS / Cowboy | Knees up | Confident stance, readiness for action, dual character interaction |
| **Medium Shot** | MS | Waist up | Natural conversational distance, balancing dialogue and body language |
| **Medium Close-Up** | MCU | Chest up | Heightened emotional engagement, standard documentary interview |
| **Close-Up** | CU | Chin to forehead | Intimate emotional connection, micro-expressions, critical revelation |
| **Extreme Close-Up** | ECU / Macro | Eyes, lips, finger on trigger, detail | Extreme tension, claustrophobia, hyper-focus on minute clue |

## 2. Camera Angles & Psychological Framing

- **Eye-Level:** Neutral, objective, empathetic, democratic relationship with audience.
- **Low Angle (Worm's Eye / Hero Shot):** Camera looks up at subject. Subject appears powerful, dominant, menacing, heroic, or towering.
- **High Angle (Bird's Eye):** Camera looks down at subject. Subject appears vulnerable, trapped, overwhelmed, or diminished.
- **Dutch Angle (Canted / Tilted):** Camera is tilted off horizontal axis. Creates psychological unease, disorientation, madness, paranoia, or impending catastrophe.
- **Over-The-Shoulder (OTS):** Grounds conversation in three-dimensional space, anchoring viewer in one character's perspective while observing another.
- **Point of View (POV):** Places the audience directly in the eyes of the character. Intensifies danger, discovery, or immersion.

## 3. Camera Movement Mechanics

| Movement Type | Motivation | Direction / Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Dolly In / Push** | Realization, intimacy, rising tension | Moves physical camera forward toward focal point |
| **Dolly Out / Pull** | Abandonment, realization of vast scope, ending | Moves physical camera backward away from focal point |
| **Pan (Horizontal)** | Follows motion, surveys scene, reveals threat | Rotates camera left/right on fixed pivot |
| **Tilt (Vertical)** | Reveals height, scale, towering architecture | Rotates camera up/down on fixed pivot |
| **Tracking / Trucking** | Dynamic energy, pace matching | Moves camera parallel alongside walking or running subject |
| **Arc / Orbit (360°)** | Isolation in time, swirling romance, entrapment | Camera circles subject while keeping them centered |
| **Crane / Jib / Drone** | Epic transition, omniscience, ascending escape | Moves camera smoothly through 3D vertical/horizontal space |
| **Whip Pan (Swish)** | High-energy transition, comedic timing, rapid time jump | Ultra-fast horizontal blur connecting two distinct scenes |
| **Steadicam / Gimbal** | Smooth exploratory wandering, dreamlike flow | Glides through corridors and spaces with human fluidity |
| **Handheld / Shaky Cam** | Urgent panic, war realism, raw documentary | Natural micro-tremor indicating visceral human operator presence |

## 4. The 180-Degree Rule & Spatial Continuity

- **The Axis of Action:** An imaginary line drawn between two interacting characters.
- **Directorial Rule:** The camera must stay on one side of this axis. Crossing the axis inverts screen direction (Character A looking right suddenly looks left), disorienting the viewer.
- **Motivated Crossings:** To cross the line without confusing the viewer:
  1. Move the camera continuously across the line during a single tracking shot.
  2. Cut to a neutral head-on shot on the line before cutting to the opposite side.
  3. Cut to a cutaway or reaction shot before transitioning to the new angle.

---

## 5. Scene Blocking: The Triangle of Actor, Camera, and Light

1. **Depth Staging:** Never place actors flat against a wall. Create foreground, midground, and background layers.
2. **Vectors of Movement:** Direct actors to move diagonally across the frame rather than strictly sideways. Diagonal movement maximizes dynamic perspective and depth.
3. **Power Shifts:** When a character gains dominance in a scene, block them to stand up, step into the key light, or move into the foreground.
